# Map 6 research: Japanese powder, snow-country villages, dusk light, and themed mountains in games

Background for **Kitsune Peak** (狐ヶ岳, *Kitsune-ga-take*): a Japanese deep-powder volcano ridden at dusk.
Written sources only (resort guides, tour write-ups, government tourism texts, reviews), paraphrased, with links
inline. No video was watched. Where sources disagree, the range is given. **[gk]** marks general knowledge we did not
verify in this pass. **[design]** marks our own proposals, not sourced facts.

## 1. Japanese powder skiing

### Why Japan gets so much light snow
- **Mechanism.** Cold Siberian air crosses the Sea of Japan, picks up moisture and is forced up by the coastal
  ranges (orographic lift). Storms run November to March
  ([Japan Uncharted, Niseko snow](https://japanuncharted.com/hokkaido/snow/niseko-snow-conditions-report)).
- **Why Hokkaido is drier than Honshu.** The cold Liman Current keeps the northern sea cooler, so the air carries
  less water. Crystals grow as needles, plates and dendrites, not rimed graupel
  ([Japan Uncharted, Hokkaido powder](https://japanuncharted.com/hokkaido/snow/hokkaido-japan-powder-snow);
  [SCMP](https://scmp.com/week-asia/lifestyle-culture/article/3276620/hokkaido-japans-famous-powder-snow-risk-global-warming-threatens-tourism-daily-life)).
- **Water content ("Japow").** Figures vary by source:
  - ~8% for Niseko, similar to Utah or Jackson Hole ([First Tracks](https://www.firsttracksonline.com/2011/10/17/skiing-japan-hakuba-niseko)).
  - 8-10% vs 15-20% for typical Alpine snow ([Wanderluxe](https://wanderluxe.theluxenomad.com/niseko-snow-forecast/)).
  - 4-6% for Hokkaido vs 8-12% for Honshu, uncited ([Japan Uncharted](https://japanuncharted.com/hokkaido/snow/hokkaido-japan-powder-snow)).
  - Cold temperatures keep it from settling for days after a storm.
- **Season.** Late November to early May; the deepest powder is late December to February.

### Niseko's gate system
- There are **11 patrol-controlled gates** on Annupuri (older sources say 9), and they are the only legal way out of
  bounds. Ducking the boundary rope is banned. Gates close in bad conditions, and the daily avalanche bulletin is
  posted at each gate. Search and rescue costs **at least ¥100,000**
  ([NISEKO Rules](https://www.niseko.ne.jp/en/niseko/niseko-rules/); [Rhythm Japan](https://rhythmjapan.com/our-stories/unlocking-niseko-gate-system)).
- Why it matters for a game: a gate is a physical, readable **threshold between groomed and wild**, and a natural
  "you are now entering the hard line" marker.

### Night skiing
- Grand Hirafu lights about **70 ha** with **1,287 metal-halide lamps**. That is enough to ride some forest and side
  runs, not just the pistes ([Powderlife](https://www.powderlife.com/blog/night-skiing-niseko-best/)).
- Lifts usually run to **20:30** (8 pm and 9 pm in some seasons), from mid-December to March, on the day ticket
  ([Wanderluxe](https://wanderluxe.theluxenomad.com/night-skiing-in-niseko/); [Holiday Niseko](https://holidayniseko.com/blog/night-skiing-niseko)).
- Look: cold-white pools of light on the piste, falling snow sparkling in the floodlight cones, and black forest
  between the pylons. Metal halide is a cool white of roughly 4,000 K and up **[gk]**.

### Terrain features and hazards
- **Mt Yotei ("Ezo Fuji").** A near-perfect conical stratovolcano, **1,898 m** high, with a summit crater **~700 m**
  across and roughly **200 m** deep (the depth comes from a travel source)
  ([JMA](https://www.data.jma.go.jp/vois/data/filing/souran_eng/volcanoes/016_yoteizan.pdf);
  [NASA Earth Observatory](https://earthobservatory.nasa.gov/images/7866/hokkaido-japan)).
  - Touring there gives about 1,200 m of vertical through spaced trees, steep canyons and alpine bowls
    ([InsideHook](https://www.insidehook.com/adventure/hokkaido-japan-ski-trip-guide)).
- **Pillows.** Deep snow mounds on boulders, stumps and ledges, usually in steep, rarely skied terrain. The danger is a
  thinly covered rock that pitches the rider forward ([SkiTalk](https://www.skitalk.com/threads/what-causes-pillows.38631/)).
- **Tree wells.** Voids under low conifer branches, observed up to **6 m** deep.
  - In two North American experiments, **90%** of volunteers placed in a tree well could not get themselves out
    ([Wikipedia: Tree well](https://en.wikipedia.com/wiki/Tree_well)).
  - The risk peaks during and just after heavy snowfall ([Ski 49N](https://ski49n.com/mountain-news/details/tree-well-safety)).
  - Foreign riders in northern Japan regularly get stuck in them
    ([PACAF](https://www.pacaf.af.mil/News/Article-Display/Article/1165167/us-airmen-rescue-thai-man-from-near-death-experience)).
- **Trees.**
  - **Todomatsu** (Sakhalin fir, *Abies sachalinensis*): about **30 m** tall, grows from sea level to ~1,650 m, and
    dominates Hokkaido's mixed forests. Its dense skirts of branches make the tree wells
    ([Trees & Shrubs Online](https://treesandshrubsonline.org/articles/abies/abies-sachalinensis/);
    [U-Tokyo repository](https://repository.dl.itc.u-tokyo.ac.jp/records/25625)).
  - **Dakekanba** (Erman's birch, *Betula ermanii*): grows at 1,500-2,500 m in the Northern Alps, above white birch
    ([MLIT: A Tale of Two Birches](https://www.mlit.go.jp/tagengo-db/en/R3-00337.html)).
    - In Hokkaido it reaches the tree line at lower altitude **[gk]**.
    - Pale peeling bark and twisted, wind-bent trunks with no low branches give open, see-through glades **[gk]**.
- **Juhyo ("snow monsters").** Firs encased in layers of wind-blown ice and snow, seen late December to late
  February. At Zao they are lit in colours at night
  ([Japan Travel](https://en.japantravel.com/event/zao-snow-monster-festival/3553)). The trees are Maries' fir
  (*Abies mariesii*, also called Aomori todomatsu) **[gk]**.
- **Wind and gullies.** At altitude, gusts over ~95 km/h build wind slabs and cornices. Lower slopes are less wind
  affected. Creek beds and steep micro-features act as terrain traps
  ([InsideHook](https://www.insidehook.com/adventure/hokkaido-japan-ski-trip-guide)).
  - Wind lips on gully walls form on the lee side ([Montana Avalanche, gully cornice example](https://www.mtavalanche.com/node/34826)).

## 2. Mountain-village and shrine architecture

### Onsen towns
- **Ginzan Onsen (Yamagata).**
  - About **12-13 ryokan** line both banks of a shallow river along a **~300 m** street.
  - The buildings are **3-4 storey** timber inns with deep wooden balconies, white plaster panels and *kote-e*
    plaster relief. Most were rebuilt after a 1913 flood, in the Taisho to early Showa period (1912 to the 1930s).
  - **Gas lamps** light the river and bridges at dusk (one guide counts 88).
  - Heavy snow sits on the eaves from late December to February
    ([Nippon.com](https://www.nippon.com/en/guide-to-japan/gu005013/soaking-in-history-at-ginzan-hot-springs.html);
    [Watabi](https://watabi.org/en/articles/ginzan-onsen-day-vs-overnight)).
- **Nyuto Onsen, Tsurunoyu (Akita).** Dates to 1638. Low **kayabuki thatched** buildings, lantern-lit hallways, and a
  milky-white sulphur outdoor bath ringed by snow, with steam rising at night
  ([Japan Uncharted](https://japanuncharted.com/akita/onsen-town/tsurunoyu-onsen-stay);
  [Japan Travel](https://en.japantravel.com/akita/nyuto-onsen-village-and-its-seven-springs-in-akita-s-mountains/71843)).
- **Gassho-zukuri (Shirakawa-go, Gokayama).**
  - The roof is about **60°**, a near-equilateral triangle that sheds snow
    ([NMNS](https://www.nmns.edu.tw/ch/learn/museum-education/naturalist/scienceimages/detail/SciencePhotography-09008/)).
    Gokayama, which gets more snow, builds steeper still ([MLIT](https://www.mlit.go.jp/tagengo-db/en/R1-00113.html)).
  - Thatch is about **35 cm** thick ([JAANUS](https://projects.mcah.columbia.edu/jaanus/record/kayabuki)); popular
    sources say up to ~90 cm. It is rethatched every 15-30 years.
- **Snow loads.** Wet snow weighs **300-500 kg/m³**, so 2 m of it on a 100 m² roof is 60-100 t. Heavy zones pile up
  **3 m or more** ([Akiya Japan](https://www.akiyajapan.com/articles/winterizing-your-akiya-what-snow-country-homes-need-that-other-regions-dont?lang=en)).
  - Roofs are shovelled by hand (*yuki-oroshi*). Where snow passes 3 m the term becomes *yuki-hori*, digging the
    house out ([Wikipedia: Snow country](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snow_country_(Japan))).
  - Modelling cue: **snow caps 0.5-1.5 m thick** that overhang the eaves, with rounded edges.

## 3. Dusk and blue-hour palette on snow

- **Sun-elevation bands.**
  - Golden hour runs from about **+6° down to -4°**.
  - Blue hour runs from **-4° to -6°**, inside civil twilight (0° to -6°), and lasts roughly **10-45 min** depending on
    latitude and season ([PetaPixel](https://petapixel.com/2014/06/11/understanding-golden-hour-blue-hour-twilights/);
    [sunrisesunset.io](https://sunrisesunset.io/golden-hour-blue-hour/)).
  - Warm yellow and orange hang on in the direction of the set sun.
- **Why the zenith is so deep a blue.** At twilight the light takes a long path through the atmosphere, and ozone's
  Chappuis bands (400-650 nm, peaks at **575 and 603 nm**) absorb yellow to red. That makes ozone the main cause of
  the twilight blue, up to about two-thirds by one estimate
  ([Wikipedia: Chappuis absorption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chappuis_absorption);
  [ACP 2023](https://acp.copernicus.org/articles/23/14829/2023/)).
- **Opposite the sun.** The dark-blue Earth's shadow rises from the horizon, with the pink **Belt of Venus** above it
  **[gk]**. On a mountain, the summit catches the last **alpenglow** (pink-orange) while the valleys are already in
  blue shade **[gk]**.
- **Why snow looks blue.** Shaded snow is lit only by the sky, so it reflects blue. Low winter sun casts long shadows,
  so most of the slope is shaded ([Story of Snow](https://www.storyofsnow.com/blog1.php/blues-and-whites-of-snow);
  [pixls.us](https://discuss.pixls.us/t/why-do-winter-landscapes-look-blue/41107)). Overcast skies flatten this to grey.
- **Lantern contrast.** Candle and gas flame sit at roughly **1,800-2,000 K**, against a blue-hour sky well above
  **10,000 K** **[gk]**. That orange-on-blue complement is what makes Ginzan Onsen photographs work.

## 4. How games give mountains an identity

- **SSX Tricky (2001).** Each course is one strong idea:
  - Garibaldi is a real Canadian peak with several routes to the finish.
  - Snowdream is a night course.
  - Mesablanca is desert reds and oranges.
  - Alaska is the most treacherous course, with near-vertical faces and cliff drops.
  - **Tokyo Megaplex** is an indoor snow park with late-night Shibuya neon. Reviews call it a pinball-inspired
    three-lap oval, with metal rails running high above the main course like a bobsled track.
  - Sources: [Giant Bomb](https://www.giantbomb.com/ssx-tricky/3030-18782/);
    [GameSpot preview](https://gamespot.com/articles/ssx-tricky-preview/1100-2801239/);
    [Gameswelt](https://www.gameswelt.de/ssx-tricky/test/ssx-tricky-3358).
  - Lesson: **one theme, one colour key, one signature gimmick per course.**
- **SSX 3 (2003).** One mountain with three peaks, each steeper than the last and each with its own look.
  - **Peak 2 has an Asian-inspired area with pagodas and temples.**
  - The whole mountain can be ridden top to bottom in about **30 min** of streamed, non-stop riding.
  - EA Radio Big's DJ talks between runs, and the music **gets louder or quieter with how well you ride**.
  - Sources: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.com/wiki/SSX3); [SSX wiki](https://ssx.fandom.com/wiki/SSX_3).
- **SSX (2012).** Real regions built from Google Earth terrain.
  - Each region ends in a **Deadly Descent**, a survival run against one hazard: trees, avalanches, drops, low
    oxygen, cold or **darkness**.
  - The Himalayas track uses chasms and wingsuit crossings.
  - Sources: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SSX_(2012_video_game));
    [Game Informer](https://gameinformer.com/games/ssx/b/xbox360/archive/2011/09/30/an-avalanche-of-ssx-screens-plus-a-trailer-and-hands-on.aspx).
  - Lesson: **give the mountain one signature hazard.** Ours is tree wells and dusk darkness.
- **Steep (Ubisoft Annecy, 2016).**
  - Designers sculpted the big landmark forms (Mont Blanc) by hand, then let procedural rules fill the rest
    ([GamesBeat](https://gamesbeat.com/how-ubisoft-built-a-mountain-and-then-created-a-video-game-around-it/)).
  - Drop zones double as challenge starts and fast travel ([Steep wiki](https://steep.fandom.com/)).
  - Road to the Olympics added a Japan map; GameSpot's review praised waist-deep powder at speed through a
    picturesque glade ([GameSpot](https://www.gamespot.com/reviews/steep-road-to-the-olympics-review/1900-6416824/)).
  - Lesson: **hand-author the silhouette and the landmarks; fill the space between them procedurally.**
- **Riders Republic (2021).**
  - Seven US parks merged into ~250 km², built from real elevation data with creative liberties.
  - "Beauty spots", memorable landmarks and purpose-built stunt spots are placed across the map
    ([Ubisoft dev blog](https://www.ubisoft.com/en-gb/game/riders-republic/news-updates/5I9IJDdoVEpxAPpdTcamo1/building-the-world-of-riders-republic);
    [GamerBraves](https://www.gamerbraves.com/?p=85108)).
  - Lesson: **landmarks are for orientation; stunt spots are authored, not found.**
- **Common pattern.**
  - The peak and one or two oversized man-made landmarks stay visible from almost everywhere.
  - Trail names carry the theme.
  - Pacing alternates tight sections (trees, village) with open release sections (bowls, flats with a view).
  - Music and ambience shift by zone.

## 5. Design takeaways for Kitsune Peak

1. **Silhouette first [design].**
   - A Yotei-like cone with a crater rim at the top. Its real proportions are ~1,900 m high with a 700 m crater, so
     scale it down to the map.
   - Keep it visible from every section as the orientation anchor, the way Steep handles Mont Blanc.
   - Put a **giant vermilion torii (~12-16 m)** on the summit ridge as the second landmark, backlit by the magenta
     horizon.
2. **Light the mountain as dusk runs downhill.** The summit catches alpenglow (sun at about +1° to 0°), mid-mountain
   is in blue hour, and the village at the bottom is lantern-lit night. Riding the course runs the clock forward.
3. **Lantern lines are the wayfinding.**
   - Tōrō (~2 m) or chōchin on posts at **15-25 m spacing** mark the intended line; a gap in the chain means a drop.
   - Warm orange on blue snow is the highest-contrast cue in the palette.
   - Red chōchin mark the main line; white ones mark the optional lines.
4. **Pillow fields as staircases.**
   - Pillows **1-3 m tall**, stepping down **2-4 m** each, spaced **5-9 m** apart along the fall line, in 3-6 drop
     chains.
   - Cap each pillow with a rounded, oversized snow mound, so any rock edge reads as a visible, avoidable hazard.
   - Landings must be deep and soft, with no speed penalty for landing flat in powder.
5. **Birch glades are open; fir stands are tight.**
   - Dakekanba glades: trunks **6-12 m** apart, no low branches, pale trunks that read against blue snow, flowy
     slalom lines.
   - Todomatsu stands: **3-6 m** apart and dark, with tree wells. They are the "expert pocket".
   - Reuse the map 2 tree-run lessons for line readability.
6. **Tree wells as the signature hazard.**
   - Show a **dark ring 1.5-3 m across** around the base of each fir.
   - Clipping the ring slows the rider sharply; a centre hit means a wipeout and respawn.
   - Never place one on the lantern line. They live only in the fir pockets, like SSX 2012's one-hazard descents.
7. **Floaty but fast powder.**
   - Visuals: board sink of about 20-40 cm, a big rooster-tail spray, a soft "whump" on landings.
   - Feel: slightly lower gravity in the air over powder, more forgiving landings, and turn drag that bleeds less
     speed than on groomers.
   - Real powder is slow; ours should feel deep without feeling like a brake.
8. **Gullies with wind lips.** Use Rusutsu's shape: ridges drop into gullies that funnel back to a run-out. Give the
   gully walls a **lee-side lip 0.5-1.5 m high** to pop off. These are the natural halfpipes.
9. **A gate threshold, Niseko style.** A rope line with a small wooden gate, a sign and a shimenawa marks the entry to
   the hard summit bowl and volcano rim. The gate shows the player that the terrain changes past this point.
10. **Volcano set-dressing.**
    - Fumarole steam vents at the crater rim (Asahidake) with drifting sulphur-yellow mist and bare dark rock patches
      where the snow melts.
    - Juhyo snow monsters (Zao) line the rim. Optionally give them faint coloured uplights.
11. **The shrine village is the jump line.**
    - A short Ginzan-style street (~150-300 m) of 2-4 storey ryokan, with **60° gassho roofs** carrying 0.5-1.5 m snow
      caps.
    - Roof-to-roof kickers, a pagoda (3 tiers, roofs scaled 10:9:8) as the centre landmark, steam from an outdoor bath
      beside the landing.
    - Kitsune statue pairs flank the start gate.
12. **Senbon-torii tunnel.** Run a fast, tight corridor of near-touching vermilion torii (about 3 m tall, about 1.5 m
    apart) through the forest. It is the map's speed and audio set piece, with a rhythmic whoosh past each gate.
13. **Pacing [design].** Open summit bowl (release), then the rim rollercoaster (rhythm), the birch weave (flow), the
    pillow chain (tension), the torii tunnel (speed), and the village jump line (finale). Alternate tight and open
    sections, never two tight ones in a row.
